# Outreach principles

Generic principles for outbound email. These are illustrative — adapt them to your
own product and audience. The drafter reads these alongside the voice guide; keep
them as durable rules of judgment rather than per-campaign tactics.

## Relevance over volume

One email that clearly belongs in the reader's inbox beats fifty that don't. If you
can't name a specific reason this person should hear from you, don't send.

## Lead with their context, not your product

Open with something true about *them* — their work, a recent move, the problem they
likely have. The product enters only after you've earned attention, and always framed
in their terms.

## One clear ask

Every email asks for exactly one thing, and the ask is easy to say yes (or no) to.
Two asks is zero asks. Make the next step small: a question, a short call, a reply.

## Earn each sentence

The job of the first sentence is to get the second one read. If a line doesn't move
the reader forward, cut it. No throat-clearing, no preamble, no "hope you're well."

## Be specific or be silent

Concrete details build trust; vague claims erode it. Prefer "you took on three new
clients this quarter" to "you're growing fast." Never claim a benefit you can't tie
to something real about the reader.

## Respect the reader's time and inbox

Short beats long. Honor unsubscribes and cooldowns without being asked. Don't
re-contact someone who hasn't replied until enough time has passed — persistence is
not the same as pressure.

## Tone matches the relationship

Cold prospects get a lighter touch and more context-setting. Existing or returning
contacts can be warmer and more direct. Never assume more familiarity than you've
earned.

## Honesty above all

Don't imply you've met when you haven't. Don't invent shared connections, fake
scarcity, or numbers you can't support. If a claim can't survive a fact-check, it
doesn't go in the email.

## Make it easy to reply

End in a way that lowers the cost of responding. A single, answerable question
outperforms a calendar link most of the time. Give the reader an easy "no" — it earns
you more real "yeses."
